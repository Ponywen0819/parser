## Abstract

*Semantic scene completion (SSC) aims to infer both the 3D geometry and semantics of a scene from single images. In contrast to prior work on SSC that heavily relies on expensive ground-truth annotations, we approach SSC in an* unsupervised *setting. Our novel method, SceneDINO, adapts techniques from self-supervised representation learning and 2D unsupervised scene understanding to SSC. Our training exclusively utilizes multi-view consistency self-supervision without any form of semantic or geometric ground truth. Given a single input image, SceneDINO infers the* 3D geometry *and expressive* 3D DINO features *in a feed-forward manner. Through a novel 3D feature distillation approach, we obtain unsupervised 3D semantics. In both 3D and 2D unsupervised scene understanding, SceneDINO reaches state-of-the-art segmentation accuracy. Linear probing our 3D features matches the segmentation accuracy of a current* supervised *SSC approach. Additionally, we showcase the domain generalization and multi-view consistency of SceneDINO, taking the first steps towards a strong foundation for single image 3D scene understanding.*

## <span id="page-0-2"></span>1. Introduction

Understanding the geometry and semantics of 3D scenes from image observations is a fundamental computer vision task with broad applications in robotics [\[26\]](#page-9-0), autonomous driving [\[46,](#page-9-1) [65\]](#page-10-0), medical image analysis [\[18,](#page-8-0) [112\]](#page-11-0), and civil engineering [\[69\]](#page-10-1). The Semantic Scene Completion (SSC) task unifies 3D geometry and semantic prediction from limited image observations [\[63,](#page-10-2) [88,](#page-11-1) [95\]](#page-11-2). Recent progress in SSC has been primarily driven by utilizing supervised learning [\[37,](#page-9-2) [87,](#page-11-3) [95\]](#page-11-2). However, acquiring large-scale 3D annotations is highly labor-intensive [\[65\]](#page-10-0). While significant resources have been invested in collecting human annotations for 2D tasks [\[52,](#page-9-3) [84\]](#page-10-3), annotating similar amounts of data in 3D remains unapproached. This motivates approaching SSC without the need for manually annotated data.

Existing SSC approaches rely on ground-truth semantic annotations and frequently utilize additional supervision from LiDAR scans [\[37,](#page-9-2) [45,](#page-9-4) [73,](#page-10-4) [95\]](#page-11-2). In contrast, we are the first to approach SSC in a *fully unsupervised* setting, *i.e*. without task supervision or other supervised components. In particular, we aim to approach SSC from a *single image* without relying on any human annotations, only learning from unlabeled multi-view images using self-supervision. This setting is extremely challenging for two reasons: *first*, the human-defined nature of semantic taxonomies is ambiguous, and *second*, a single image only provides a partial observation of the scene with many invisible areas. We take inspiration from recent advances in self-supervised learning (SSL) of 2D representations and 3D reconstruction. 2D SSL representations, such as from DINO [\[11\]](#page-8-1), have been shown effective for 2D unsupervised scene understanding [\[32,](#page-9-5) [103\]](#page-11-4). 3D reconstruction approaches successfully leveraged SSL from multi-view data to infer dense 3D geometry from a single image [\[33,](#page-9-6) [107\]](#page-11-5).

In this paper, we present *SceneDINO*, to the best of our knowledge, the first approach for unsupervised semantic scene completion. Trained using 2D SSL features

## <span id="page-1-1"></span>2. Related Work

Single-image scene reconstruction. Estimating 3D geometry from image observations is a fundamental task in computer vision and has been studied for decades [\[36\]](#page-9-7). Traditional approaches, such as structure from motion [\[89\]](#page-11-6), as well as recent neural radiance fields (NeRFs) [\[74\]](#page-10-5), perform scene reconstruction using multiple images, as reviewed by multiple surveys [\[34,](#page-9-8) [108,](#page-11-7) [119\]](#page-12-0). Recently, estimating dense 3D geometry from a single image have been approached [\[8,](#page-8-2) [33,](#page-9-6) [80,](#page-10-6) [85,](#page-10-7) [96,](#page-11-8) [102,](#page-11-9) [107,](#page-11-5) [113\]](#page-11-10). Unlike monocular depth estimation [\[75\]](#page-10-8), these approaches predict the depth for visible and occluded regions, reconstructing a complete scene. Behind the Scenes (BTS) [\[107\]](#page-11-5) introduced an approach for unsupervised single-image *scene* reconstruction using multi-view self-supervision, which infers dense 3D geometry in a feed-forward manner. Our approach extends BTS by additionally lifting self-supervised features into 3D for unsupervised 3D scene understanding.

Semantic scene completion (SSC), also known as 3D semantic occupancy prediction, aims to jointly estimate the 3D geometry and semantics of a scene [\[62,](#page-10-9) [63,](#page-10-2) [95,](#page-11-2) [117\]](#page-12-1). Initial approaches used 3D semantic and geometric annotations and addressed indoor scenes [\[6,](#page-8-3) [13,](#page-8-4) [57](#page-10-10)[–59,](#page-10-11) [67,](#page-10-12) [116\]](#page-12-2), outdoor scenes with LiDAR [\[16,](#page-8-5) [61,](#page-10-13) [86,](#page-11-11) [87,](#page-11-3) [109\]](#page-11-12), or both domains [\[8,](#page-8-2) [73\]](#page-10-4). Using birds-eye views has been proven effective for SSC [\[44,](#page-9-9) [64,](#page-10-14) [99\]](#page-11-13). To overcome the need for 3D annotations, approaches for using 2D annotations have been proposed [\[37,](#page-9-2) [45,](#page-9-4) [81\]](#page-10-15). While SelfOcc [\[45\]](#page-9-4) and RenderOcc [\[81\]](#page-10-15) use multiple inference views, S4C [\[37\]](#page-9-2) performs single-image SSC. In particular, S4C [\[37\]](#page-9-2) employs a supervised 2D model and lifts 2D multi-view semantic predictions into 3D. In contrast to using 2D annotations, GaussTR [\[48\]](#page-9-10) uses 2D foundation models for SSC and multiple views during inference. However, GaussTR relies on heavily supervised foundation models, including SAM [\[52\]](#page-9-3) and Metric3Dv2 [\[42\]](#page-9-11), and uses weak supervision from image/text pairs. To the best of our knowledge, there is no method for approaching SSC without the need for any ground-truth annotations. Our work presents the first unsupervised SSC approach, utilizing lifted SSL features and a single RGB input image for inference.

Self-supervised representation learning (SSL) aims to extract general features from data without annotations, facilitating various downstream tasks such as segmentation [\[24\]](#page-8-6). Recent SSL methods, often based on Vision Transformers (ViTs) [\[23\]](#page-8-7), leverage clustering [\[2,](#page-8-8) [9,](#page-8-9) [10,](#page-8-10) [47,](#page-9-12) [60\]](#page-10-16), masked modeling [\[20,](#page-8-11) [29,](#page-9-13) [39,](#page-9-14) [76,](#page-10-17) [106\]](#page-11-14), contrastive learning [\[3,](#page-8-12) [12,](#page-8-13) [14,](#page-8-14) [38,](#page-9-15) [40,](#page-9-16) [41\]](#page-9-17), or negative-free [\[4,](#page-8-15) [5,](#page-8-16) [11,](#page-8-1) [28,](#page-9-18) [79\]](#page-10-18) pretext tasks [\[22,](#page-8-17) [78\]](#page-10-19) for large-scale training. Stateof-the-art models, *e.g*., DINO [\[11\]](#page-8-1), produce semantically rich, dense features, driving recent advances in 2D unsupervised scene understanding [\[32,](#page-9-5) [103\]](#page-11-4). We here aim to bring expressive features from DINO [\[11,](#page-8-1) [79\]](#page-10-18) to 3D for SSC.

2D-to-3D feature lifting. The expressiveness of 2D visual representations has motivated lifting 2D features into 3D [\[93,](#page-11-15) [110\]](#page-11-16). Existing approaches utilize multi-view 2D features for 3D feature lifting [\[30,](#page-9-19) [43,](#page-9-20) [49,](#page-9-21) [53,](#page-9-22) [72,](#page-10-20) [82,](#page-10-21) [92,](#page-11-17) [93,](#page-11-15) [97,](#page-11-18) [100,](#page-11-19) [101,](#page-11-20) [105,](#page-11-21) [110,](#page-11-16) [115\]](#page-12-3). Lifting 2D features is effective in various tasks, including few-shot semantic occupancy prediction [\[110\]](#page-11-16), and refining 2D representations [\[115\]](#page-12-3). However, existing feature-lifting approaches fit to a single scene [\[49,](#page-9-21) [53,](#page-9-22) [92,](#page-11-17) [93,](#page-11-15) [100,](#page-11-19) [101,](#page-11-20) [110,](#page-11-16) [115\]](#page-12-3), require RGB-D inputs [\[30,](#page-9-19) [43,](#page-9-20) [72,](#page-10-20) [97,](#page-11-18) [105\]](#page-11-21), or work on 3D point cloud inputs [\[82\]](#page-10-21). The only feed-forward approaches that use RGB inputs and lift 2D features, which we are aware of, are GaussTR [\[48\]](#page-9-10); MVSplat360 [\[15\]](#page-8-18). However, both approaches utilize multiple input images during inference, and MVSplat360 [\[15\]](#page-8-18) only predicts low-dimensional feature representations, which are not suitable for unsupervised scene understanding. In contrast, we propose the first feedforward approach for inferring lifted high-dimensional and rich 3D features using a single input image.

2D unsupervised semantic segmentation partitions images automatically into semantically meaningful regions without any form of human annotations. Early deep learning-based methods [\[17,](#page-8-19) [35,](#page-9-23) [47\]](#page-9-12) approach the problem via representation learning. Leveraging SSL features from DINO as an inductive prior, STEGO [\[32\]](#page-9-5) distills the feature representation into a lower-dimensional space for unsupervised probing. ## <span id="page-2-5"></span>3. Unsupervised Semantic Scene Completion

We approach semantic scene completion (SSC) without any form of manual supervision. To this end, we first describe SceneDINO, predicting *3D geometry* and expressive *3D features* from a *single image* in a *feed-forward manner* (Sec. [3.1\)](#page-2-1), and SceneDINO's multi-view training (Sec. [3.2\)](#page-3-0). Next, we present our 3D feature distillation approach to obtain *unsupervised 3D semantic* predictions (Sec. [3.3\)](#page-3-1). An overview of our full pipeline, including inference, rendering, and multi-view self-supervision, is provided in Fig. [2.](#page-2-0)

Notation. Let I<sup>0</sup> ∈ [0, 1]3×H×<sup>W</sup> be a single RGB input image (for both training & inference) with corresponding pose T<sup>0</sup> ∈ R 4×4 and projection matrix K<sup>0</sup> ∈ R 3×4 . For training, let (Iv, Tv, Kv) with v ∈ {1, 2, . . . , n}, be n additional views for multi-view self-supervision. Assuming a pinhole camera model, any 3D point x ∈ R 3 in world coordinates can be projected onto the image plane of view v and the input view v = 0 with the perspective projection πv(x).

#### <span id="page-2-1"></span>3.1. SceneDINO

Given a single input image I0, SceneDINO represents the dense geometric structure and features of a scene as a continuous mapping from world coordinates x ∈ R 3 to a volumetric density σ<sup>x</sup> ∈ R<sup>+</sup> and a feature f<sup>x</sup> ∈ R <sup>D</sup>. This continuous output representation is often called a *feature field*. While SceneDINO could represent any feature space, we aim for expressive SSL features from DINO [\[11,](#page-8-1) [79\]](#page-10-18).

Architecture & feature field inference. Our SceneDINO architecture comprises two main parts: a 2D encoderdecoder ξ and an MLP decoder (*cf* . Fig. [2a\)](#page-2-0), following BTS [\[107\]](#page-11-5). ξ takes in I<sup>0</sup> and produces a per-pixel embedding E ∈ R <sup>D</sup>E×H×<sup>W</sup> with D<sup>E</sup> dimensions. Intuitively, every spatial element of E represents a camera ray through a pixel, capturing both local geometry and features.

To infer the feature at a 3D position x, we employ a two-layer MLP decoder ϕ (*cf* . Fig. [2a\)](#page-2-0). Given a position x within the camera frustum, we project x into the camera plane, obtaining the pixel location u = π0(x). We query E at the position u using bilinear interpolation, obtaining the local embedding eu. Based on the embedding eu, the pixel position u, and the distance d<sup>x</sup> ∈ R<sup>+</sup> of x to the camera, we obtain the density σ<sup>x</sup> and feature prediction f<sup>x</sup> as

<span id="page-2-2"></span>
$$
(\sigma_{\mathbf{x}}, f_{\mathbf{x}}) = \phi(\mathbf{e}_{\mathbf{u}}, \gamma(\mathbf{u}, d_{\mathbf{x}})),
$$
 (1)

where γ denotes a positional encoding [\[74\]](#page-10-5).

Feature, depth & color volume rendering. SceneDINO predicts a continuous feature field from a single image. This representation can be used to render features and depth in 2D from an arbitrary viewpoint (*cf* . Fig. [2b\)](#page-2-0), following the discretization strategy of Max *et al*. [\[71\]](#page-10-22). Given a viewpoint (Tr, Kr), we sample L points x<sup>i</sup> along the ray through pixel ur, with distance δ<sup>i</sup> between x<sup>i</sup> and xi+1. Based on the volumetric densities σ<sup>x</sup><sup>i</sup> (*cf* . Eq. [1\)](#page-2-2), we can compute the probabilities α<sup>i</sup> of the ray ending between x<sup>i</sup> and xi+1, and accumulate these into V<sup>i</sup> , the probability of x<sup>i</sup> being visible:

$$
V_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1} (1 - \alpha_j), \quad \text{with } \alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_{\mathbf{x}_i} \delta_i). \tag{2}
$$

Using V<sup>i</sup> and α<sup>i</sup> , we render depth ˜d<sup>u</sup><sup>r</sup> and feature ˜f<sup>u</sup><sup>r</sup> from the estimated features f<sup>x</sup><sup>i</sup> from Eq. [\(1\)](#page-2-2) and distances d<sup>x</sup><sup>i</sup> to x<sup>i</sup> onto the image plane at position u<sup>r</sup> as

<span id="page-2-3"></span>
$$
\tilde{f}_{\mathbf{u}_r} = \sum_{i=1}^{L} V_i \alpha_i f_{\mathbf{x}_i} \qquad \tilde{d}_{\mathbf{u}_r} = \sum_{i=1}^{L} V_i \alpha_i d_{\mathbf{x}_i}.
$$
 (3)

<span id="page-3-3"></span>The differentiability of this rendering process enables us to self-supervise SceneDINO using multi-view images and their 2D feature representations (*e.g*., from DINO [\[11\]](#page-8-1)). SceneDINO predicts 3D geometry and features, but does not predict color as we focus on semantic downstream tasks. To obtain color for image reconstruction during training, we follow the color sampling approach of BTS [\[107\]](#page-11-5).

#### <span id="page-3-0"></span>3.2. 3D feature field training

We train SceneDINO using *multi-view self-supervision* (*cf* . Fig. [2c\)](#page-2-0), aiming to obtain an expressive and view-consistent feature field without the need for any form of manual annotations. For self-supervision, we sample n + 1 views I<sup>v</sup> with camera parameters[1](#page-0-0) Tv, K<sup>v</sup> from the data and obtain dense 2D features F<sup>v</sup> from a self-supervised ViT (*e.g*., DINO [\[11\]](#page-8-1)). Note that the 2D features entail a resolution of F<sup>v</sup> ∈ R D× <sup>H</sup> <sup>p</sup> <sup>×</sup> <sup>W</sup> <sup>p</sup> , due to the ViT patch size p. The set of training views and features V = {(Iv, Tv, Kv, Fv) | v = 0, . . . , n} is randomly partitioned into two subsets Vsource and Vtarget. Training reconstructs the views Vtarget using the views of Vsource. In practice, we use a randomly sampled set of image patches that align with the ViT patches instead of the full image. In the following, we still refer to images for the sake of brevity.

Image reconstruction. We aim to learn the geometry of our feature field via multi-view photometric consistency. In particular, for every image I<sup>t</sup> ∈ Vtarget we derive a reconstructed image ˆIt,s from every view s in Vsource using differentiable rendering and color sampling. Equipped with both the reconstructed image ˆIt,s and the target image It, we compute our photometric loss per patch as

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\rm p} = \min_{\mathbf{I}_s \in \mathbb{V}_{\rm source}} \Big( \lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_1(\mathbf{I}_t, \hat{\mathbf{I}}_{t,s}) + \lambda_{\rm SSIM} \mathcal{L}_{\rm SSIM}(\mathbf{I}_t, \hat{\mathbf{I}}_{t,s}) \Big).
$$
 (4)

We only consider the minimum per-patch loss across the different views in Vsource. The scalars λ<sup>1</sup> and λSSIM weight the absolute error L<sup>1</sup> and the SSIM loss LSSIM [\[104\]](#page-11-24).

To regularize the 3D geometry prediction, we impose smoothness using an edge-aware smoothness loss [\[27\]](#page-9-26). Based on the estimated depth ˜d<sup>u</sup><sup>t</sup> (*cf* . Eq. [3\)](#page-2-3), we obtain the inverse and mean-normalized depth ˜d ∗ u<sup>t</sup> . Using ˜d ∗ u<sup>t</sup> , we compute the edge-aware smoothness L<sup>s</sup> for pixel u<sup>t</sup> as

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\rm s} = |\nabla_x \, \tilde{d}_{\mathbf{u}_t}^*| \, e^{-|\nabla_x \mathbf{I}_t|} + |\nabla_y \, \tilde{d}_{\mathbf{u}_t}^*| \, e^{-|\nabla_y \mathbf{I}_t|}, \qquad (5)
$$

using the first spatial derivatives ∇<sup>x</sup> and ∇<sup>y</sup> at ut.

As we aim to learn a high-resolution (continuous) feature field, we render 2D features using Eq. [3](#page-2-3) at the full image resolution Fˆ <sup>t</sup> ∈ R <sup>D</sup>×H×<sup>W</sup>. #### <span id="page-3-1"></span>3.3. 3D feature distillation for unsupervised SSC

Given the expressive feature field representation, we aim to obtain unsupervised semantic predictions for SSC. While na¨ıve k-means [\[68,](#page-10-23) [70\]](#page-10-24) can yield meaningful pseudo semantics, distilling features into a lower-dimensional space has been shown to be more effective in 2D semantic segmentation [\[32,](#page-9-5) [54\]](#page-9-27). To this end, we present a novel 3D feature distillation approach (*cf* . Fig. [3\)](#page-3-2). We train a pointwise segmentation head h, mapping f<sup>x</sup> ∈ R <sup>D</sup> to a lowerdimensional distilled representation z<sup>x</sup> ∈ R <sup>K</sup>, with K ≪ D. The resulting distilled space is clustered to obtain pseudosemantic predictions p<sup>x</sup> ∈ [0, 1]<sup>C</sup>, with C pseudo classes.

Existing work in 2D unsupervised semantic segmentation has shown that SSL feature correspondence captures

<span id="page-4-2"></span>semantic class co-occurence [\[32\]](#page-9-5). This correspondence between two batches of N sample points X = [x1, . . . , xN] and Y = [y1, . . . , yN] can be expressed by pairwise feature similarity Si,j = cos-sim(fx<sup>i</sup> , fy<sup>j</sup> ) ∈ [−1, 1]. Similarly, we can express the correspondence in the distilled feature space by S h i,j = cos-sim(h(fx<sup>i</sup> ), h(fy<sup>j</sup> )) ∈ [−1, 1]. We describe the sampling of the x<sup>i</sup> and y<sup>j</sup> below.

Feature distillation. We aim to distill features such that similar features align while dissimilar features are separated. To this end, we use the contrastive correlation loss Lcorr, introduced by STEGO [\[32\]](#page-9-5) and defined as

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{corr}}(f_{\mathbf{X}}, f_{\mathbf{Y}}, b) = -\sum_{i,j} (S_{i,j} - b) \max(S_{i,j}^h, 0), \quad (8)
$$

where fX, f<sup>Y</sup> are the features of the two sample batches. This loss pushes S h i,j towards 1 in case Si,j exceeds the threshold b. Otherwise, Lcorr pushes the S h i,j below 0.

The correlation loss Lcorr requires informative pairs of sampled features, balancing attractive and repulsive signals. Following STEGO [\[32\]](#page-9-5), we consider three different relations: *(1)* feature pairs from the same image (fX, fX), *(2)* feature pairs from an image and its k-nearest neighbors in feature space (fX, fYkNN ), and *(3)* feature pairs from an image and a randomly sampled other image (fX, fYrand ). Note that each pair is obtained from SceneDINO's 3D feature field, see below. Equipped with the three feature sample pairs, we compute the full distillation loss as

<span id="page-4-0"></span>
$$
\mathcal{L}_{dist} = \lambda_{self} \mathcal{L}_{corr}(f_{\mathbf{X}}, f_{\mathbf{X}}, b_{self}) \n+ \lambda_{kNN} \mathcal{L}_{corr}(f_{\mathbf{X}}, f_{\mathbf{Y}_{kNN}}, b_{kNN}) \n+ \lambda_{rand} \mathcal{L}_{corr}(f_{\mathbf{X}}, f_{\mathbf{Y}_{rand}}, b_{rand}),
$$
\n(9)

where λself, λkNN, and λrand denote the scalar loss weights. bself, bkNN, and brand are the contrastive thresholds.

Feature sampling in 3D. While obtaining feature pairs using 2D rendered features is straightforward [\[32\]](#page-9-5), we aim to take advantage of our learned 3D geometry of the scene. To this end, we introduce a novel 3D feature sampling approach for the distillation loss Ldist from Eq. [\(9\)](#page-4-0). Our goal is to sample features both similar and dissimilar in terms of the encoded semantic concept, which should capture *rich semantics* as well as *different semantic concepts*.

First, we obtain all G visible 3D surface points V ∈ R <sup>3</sup>×<sup>G</sup> and their depth d<sup>V</sup> ∈ R G <sup>+</sup> from the camera. To sample points that cover different semantic concepts, we use depth as a cue and sample different depth ranges. In particular, we sort the surface points V based on dV. The sorted surface points Vˆ are partitioned into M equally-sized chunks; we uniformly sample a single 3D point from each chunk, resulting in M center points X ∈ R <sup>3</sup>×<sup>M</sup>.

Equipped with the center points X, we aim to extract rich semantic features from the feature field. While we could just obtain the features for X, we query positions in the

<span id="page-4-1"></span>![](_page_4_Figure_10.jpeg)

Figure 4. 3D feature sampling. We first sample a center point X<sup>i</sup> from all visible surface points. Further points are sampled within the radius r around the center point Xi. neighborhood of X to increase semantic richness and better capture the 3D structure of the scene for distillation. In particular, for each center point, we randomly sample a point within a radius of r = 0.5 m. To account for samples falling into unoccupied regions in our feature field, we only keep samples with a sufficient density σ > 0.5. We repeat this sampling process until we obtain N valid samples per center point. Using these samples, we query our feature field, resulting in a feature batch f<sup>X</sup> ∈ R <sup>D</sup>×<sup>N</sup> for each of the G center points in each scene (*cf* . Fig. [4\)](#page-4-1).

To obtain fYkNN and fYrand , we utilize a feature buffer that efficiently stores the sampled features of multiple scenes. Given a new input image, we obtain G feature batches f<sup>X</sup> as just described. For each fX, we randomly sample another feature batch from the buffer to obtain fYrand . To obtain fYkNN , we search in the feature buffer for the k-nearest neighbors of fX, using the average feature of each batch. From these k-nearest neighbors, we randomly pick a feature batch to obtain fYkNN and compute the distillation loss Ldist. After repeating this process for each of the current G feature batches, we add the current feature batches to the feature buffer and remove the oldest batches.

Unsupervised probing. To obtain semantic predictions, we probe the distilled feature space using k-means [\[68,](#page-10-23) [70\]](#page-10-24). In particular, we iteratively update cluster centers θ ∈ R <sup>K</sup>×<sup>C</sup> using cosine distance-based mini-batch kmeans [\[90\]](#page-11-26) during distillation. To infer the final semantic prediction, we compute p<sup>x</sup> = softmax(cos-sim(h(fx), θ)).

## <span id="page-4-3"></span>4. Experiments

We evaluate SceneDINO on SSC and compare it to a simple unsupervised SSC baseline (Sec. [4.1\)](#page-5-0). We also report results for 2D unsupervised segmentation, including domain generalization results (Sec. [4.2\)](#page-6-0). Finally, we explore multiview feature consistency (Sec. [4.3\)](#page-6-1) and present an analysis of SceneDINO and our 3D distillation (Sec. [4.4\)](#page-7-0).

Datasets. We train using KITTI-360 [\[65\]](#page-10-0), composed of clips from a moving vehicle equipped with cameras. For <span id="page-5-2"></span>consistency, we follow S4C [\[37\]](#page-9-2) by sampling eight views and using the dataset camera poses. We also provide results with estimated poses. We also show experiments for training on RealEstate10k [\[118\]](#page-12-4), composed of monocular videos. Here, we follow the setup of BTS [\[107\]](#page-11-5), obtaining three views. If not noted differently, we report results obtained with training on KITTI-360. For SSC and 2D semantic segmentation validation, we use the SSCBench-KITTI-360 test split [\[63\]](#page-10-2). Cityscapes [\[19\]](#page-8-22) and BDD100K [\[114\]](#page-11-27) val are used for domain generalization results. To enable evaluation in 3D and 2D, we use the 19-class taxonomy of Cityscapes and perform 2D evaluation on Cityscapes, BDD100K, and KITTI-360 on 19 classes. For SSCBench, we combine classes to adhere to the 15 SSCBench classes.

3D evaluation. Given our unsupervised setup, we predict pseudo-semantic classes that must be aligned with the ground truth for evaluation. We follow standard practice in 2D unsupervised semantic segmentation [\[17,](#page-8-19) [31,](#page-9-24) [32,](#page-9-5) [50,](#page-9-25) [91,](#page-11-22) [94\]](#page-11-23) by applying Hungarian matching [\[56\]](#page-10-25) to align our pseudo semantics. For validating the aligned semantics, we follow the standardized SSCBench [\[63\]](#page-10-2) protocol and report both semantic performance using the mean Intersectionover-Union (mIoU) and geometric performance using IoU, precision, and recall. We report all metrics on SSCBench ranges 12.8 m, 25.6 m, and 51.2 m.

2D evaluation. Following the established evaluation protocol in 2D unsupervised semantic segmentation [\[17,](#page-8-19) [31,](#page-9-24) [32,](#page-9-5) [50,](#page-9-25) [91,](#page-11-22) [94\]](#page-11-23), we use the all-pixel accuracy (Acc) and mean Intersection-over-Union (mIoU) metrics. Likewise, in line with prior work, 2D segmentation predictions of all models are refined using a dense Conditional Random Field [\[55\]](#page-10-26) before computing Acc and mIoU.

Multi-view feature consistency evaluation. We aim to evaluate the multi-view consistency of our feature field. As we are not aware of any general feed-forward 3D feature field approach, we compare against 2D SSL models. To measure multi-view consistency in 2D, we use two video frames and estimate optical flow and occlusions with RAFT [\[98\]](#page-11-28). We backward warp 2D features of the second frame to the first. On the aligned features, we compute the feature similarity using absolute error (L1), the Euclidean distance (L2), and the cosine similarity, ignoring occlusions.

Implementation details. Our encoder-decoder uses a DINO-B/8 [\[11\]](#page-8-1) backbone and a dense prediction decoder [\[83\]](#page-10-29). If not stated differently, our target features are obtained from DINO-B/8 [\[11\]](#page-8-1). We train using a batch size of 4 and extract 32 patches of size 8 × 8 from each image to compute LSceneDINO. Volume rendering samples each ray at L = 32 uniformly spaced points in inverse depth within [3 m, 80 m]. We train for 100 k steps using Adam [\[51\]](#page-9-28) with a base learning rate of 10<sup>−</sup><sup>4</sup> . Training takes ca. 2 days on a *single* V100 GPU. We distill using a batch size of 4, 5 center points, a feature batch of size 576, and cluster with K = 19. For kNN sampling, we use k = 4. The feature buffer holds 256 feature batches. ## 5. Conclusion

We presented SceneDINO, to our knowledge, the first approach for unsupervised semantic scene completion. Trained using multi-view images and 2D DINO features without human supervision, SceneDINO is able to predict an expressive 3D feature field using a single input image during inference. Our novel 3D distillation approach yields state-of-the-art results in unsupervised SSC. While we focus on unsupervised SSC, our multi-view feature consistency, linear probing, and domain generalization results highlight the potential of SceneDINO as a strong foundation for various 3D scene-understanding tasks.

#### A.1. Implementation details

We implement SceneDINO in PyTorch [\[122\]](#page-19-0) and build on the code of BTS [\[107\]](#page-11-5), STEGO [\[32\]](#page-9-5), and S4C [\[37\]](#page-9-2). Our encoder-decoder (pre-trained DINO-B/8 and randomly initialized dense prediction decoder) produces per-pixel embeddings of dimensionality D<sup>E</sup> = 256. Based on these embeddings, the two-layer MLP ϕ (hidden dimension 128) predicts 64 features. As rendering features is expensive, requiring multiple forward passes through the MLP, ϕ predicts 64 features. We employ another MLP to up-project again to the full dimensionality D = 768, this MLP is learn with SceneDINO and can up-project both 3D features and 2D rendered features. We train for 100 k steps with a base learning rate of 10<sup>−</sup><sup>4</sup> , dropping to 10<sup>−</sup><sup>5</sup> after 50 k steps. We train using a batch size of 4, extracting 32 patches of size 8 × 8 per image. These patches align with the per-patch DINO target features. For our feature field loss formulation (*cf* . Sec. [3.2\)](#page-3-0), we use the loss weights λ<sup>p</sup> = 1, λ<sup>s</sup> = 0.001, λ<sup>f</sup> = 0.2, λfs = 0.25.

The MLP head h (hidden dimension 768) produces 64 distilled features. We perform distillation for 1000 steps with a learning rate of 5 · 10<sup>−</sup><sup>4</sup> . We train using a batch size of 4, 5 center points, a feature batch of size 576, and cluster with K = 19. For kNN sampling, we use k = 4. The feature buffer holds 256 feature batches. The loss term in Eq. [\(9\)](#page-4-0) is parameterized with λself = 0.08 λkNN = 0.43 λrand = 0.67, and brand = 0.87. For the similarity thresholds we use bself = 0.44, bkNN = 0.18, and brand = 0.87.

We follow standard practice in 2D unsupervised semantic segmentation [\[17,](#page-8-19) [31,](#page-9-24) [32,](#page-9-5) [50,](#page-9-25) [77,](#page-10-27) [91,](#page-11-22) [94\]](#page-11-23) by applying Hungarian matching [\[56\]](#page-10-25) to align our pseudo semantics. For SSC validation, we map down to 15 semantic classes while following existing work [\[31,](#page-9-24) [32\]](#page-9-5) for 2D validation and map to 19 semantic classes.

#### A.2. Datasets

We provide additional details about the datasets utilized to train and evaluate SceneDINO.

KITTI-360 [\[63,](#page-10-2) [65\]](#page-10-0) provides video sequences from a moving vehicle equipped with a forward-facing stereo pair and two side-facing fisheye cameras. In future frames, the fisheye views capture additional geometric and semantic cues of regions occluded in the forward-facing view. For training, we resample the fisheye images into perspective projection. We focus on an area approximately 50 meters ahead of the ego vehicle. Assuming an average velocity of 30−50 km/h, side views are randomly sampled 1–4 seconds into the future. Given a frame rate of 10 Hz, this translates to 10 – 40 time steps. Each training sample consists of eight images: four forward-facing views (including the input image) and four side-facing views.

To evaluate our predicted field in SSCBench-KITTI-360, we follow the evaluation procedure of S4C [\[37\]](#page-9-2). The voxel predictions are evaluated in three different ranges: 12.8 m × 12.8 m × 6.4 m, 25.6 m × 25.6 m × 6.4 m, and the full range 51.2 m × 51.2 m × 6.4 m. For each voxel, multiple evenly distributed points are sampled from the semantic field. The predictions are aggregated per voxel by taking the maximum occupancy and weighting the class predictions accordingly.

Cityscapes [\[19\]](#page-8-22) consists of 500 high-resolution and densely annotated validation images of ego-centric driving scenes. For validation, Cityscapes uses a 19-class taxonomy. We leverage the Cityscapes validation samples at a resolution of 640 × 192 for our domain generalization experiments (2D semantic segmentation).

#### A.3. Computational complexity

SceneDINO requires only a *single* GPU for training and inference. In SSCBench (51.2 m range), SceneDINO requires 0.76±0.1 s to infer a full scene on a V100 GPU. The peak VRAM usage during inference is 11 GB. For reference, S4C requires 0.32±0.13 s. Considering our expressive and high-dimensional feature field and ViT encoder, this is a moderate runtime increase. SceneDINO has 100 M parameters and is trained for approximately 2 days on a *single* V100 32 GB GPU. All results are reported using automatic mixed precision.

## B. Multi-View Feature Consistency Evaluation

We aim to measure the multi-view consistency of 2D and 3D features. Note, we are not aware of any standardized approach for multi-view feature consistency. To this end, we employ a straightforward approach. Given two video frames with a temporal stride of 3, forward optical flow is computed using RAFT large [\[98\]](#page-11-28). We estimate occlusion by forward-backward consistency [\[124\]](#page-19-1); for this, we also compute the backward optical flow. The 2D feature maps obtained using the second frame are backward warped to the features of the first frame. We compute different similarity metrics between the aligned features (L1, L2, and cos-sim). Note that we ignore occlusions. While features from DINO, DINOv2, and FiT3D possess a lower resolution than our 2D rendered SceneDINO features, we upscale these features to the image resolution before warping. This evaluation approach utilizes optical flow correspondences and captures both ego motion as well as object motion, offering a simple way to evaluate multi-view feature consistency.

## D. Limitations and Future Work

Target features. Our method builds on DINO [\[11\]](#page-8-1) to obtain target features. While we learn to lift these features into 3D and improve multi-view feature consistency, we cannot improve the discriminative power of the target features *per se*. However, SceneDINO can be trained using arbitrary 2D target features and can profit from future advances in SSL

<span id="page-18-2"></span>![](_page_18_Picture_6.jpeg)

Figure 10. 2D SceneDINO features on out-of-domain images. We visualize our 2D rendered features *(right)* given an out-ofdomain image *(left)* from ADE20K [\[126\]](#page-19-3). We use the first three principal components for feature visualization. While not trained on such scenes, SceneDINO still produces plausible feature maps.

representation. Note that training SceneDINO requires only 2 days on a single GPU and our training transfers seamlessly to different target features (*e.g*., DINOv2), thus, utilizing SceneDINO differently is straightforward.

Dynamic objects. Our loss does not model dynamic objects and relies on a static scene assumption. This can potentially cause inaccurate predictions for dynamic classes such as *person* in our experiments. Recent works in depth estimation have explicitly modeled the probability of areas being dynamic [\[125\]](#page-19-4) and even their motion within the scene [\[123\]](#page-19-5), which might be extended to SceneDINO.

View sampling and camera poses. For sampling views during training, we rely on the sampling scheme of S4C [\[37\]](#page-9-2). This is not directly applicable to other nondriving datasets, where the sampling needs to be tuned. In addition, our approach requires accurate camera poses for each view. We demonstrated that these can be obtained in an unsupervised way for KITTI-360 (*cf* . Tab. [5](#page-7-2) & Tab. [12\)](#page-18-1). However, obtaining unsupervised camera poses in more challenging scenarios and conditions is still challenging [\[120\]](#page-18-3).

Future work. SceneDINO is only trained using a single dataset to be comparable to existing SSC approaches. However, scaling our approach to multiple datasets of more variable scenes could lead to more general feature representations. Ultimately, scaling SceneDINO to internet-scale videos might enable strong zero-shot and cross-domain 3D scene understanding.
